A few words about the typeface

by Sebastian Boroș

This is Guapísima Pro, a display typeface designed for titles, mastheads, social media posts, book covers, logos, packaging and whichever project that requires bold elegance. Think Caslon Graphique on steroids, with a dash of 18th-century Spanish baroque: letters with vertical and oblique contrast, tight-but-not-touching spacing, and a wardrobe of typographic niceties. Ligatures, small caps, old-style figures, tabular figures, small-caps figures, fractions, everything is here. Now your punchy words can be dramatic and typographically sophisticated.

It is a love letter to the centuries-old tradition of the Gros Canon, the large display romans that emerged in Paris around 1530 with the type attributed to Maître Constantin.

The Story

I got into one of the last available places in Dr. Frank Blokland’s 2024–2025 Expert class Type design, the last year he held the course at the Plantin Institute of Typography in Antwerp. I still think of it as a great stroke of luck. Frank has been one of my biggest type heroes since forever, and after at least 15 years of poring over the dtl website, the first days in Antwerp felt a bit surreal.

One of the assignments of the course was to pick a personal project, either a revival or some kind of type-related research. I went for a revival, based on the work of Gerónimo Antonio Gil, an 18th-century Spanish engraver and punch cutter. His story begins at a good moment: The Enlightenment was in full swing in Spain, and book production was high on the list of reforms of King Charles iii. For the types of the Royal Printing House of Madrid (La Imprenta Real), Gil was appointed to supervise and cut what would become one of the first comprehensive collections of Spanish movable types.

Gil was surely aware of the work of his peers from Northern Europe, such as Jacques François Rosart or Johan Michael Fleischmann, but while his types carry over some details of Dutch origin, their generous widths and the pronounced oblique contrast, among other more discreet details, make them quite original. The revival of Gil’s types is a project that I plan to finish and release in 2027, under the name Hispanica. In the meantime, you can find out more about it here.

I mentioned Gil’s work because that was what sparked the idea for Guapísima. How would a fat version of that lovely Canon Chico look like, but in a more contemporary tone? Some may notice in Guapísima an echo of the 70s advertising types and my answer is: yes, there could very well be one. The end of the 19th century and the beginning of the 20th saw the emergence of fat types based on the modern, vertical-contrast types (for example, Century Bold or Poster Bodoni) and also on Renaissance models (Cooper Black). Maybe the graphic designers in the 70s wanted to explore a less-beaten path of type inflation: the fat baroques. Who knows.

As Frank’s research suggests, Gutenberg (and, a bit later, the Renaissance punch cutters) based their types on a standardized system of units. It sped up production, spacing and typesetting. Imagine setting a justified page of metal type. If every character in the font, even the spaces, is built on a grid of units, then at the end of a line you can tell at a glance whether you need two, three, five or any other number of units to add between the words for a neat right edge. More about Frank’s research here.

This systematic approach was a powerful production tool in the metal type era, and it is just as valuable for teaching and producing digital type. The grid’s units are divisions of the vertical stroke of the n, so every width and space traces back to a single shape. Eyeballing means tedious back-and-forth adjustments; a unitized design can be adjusted quickly. Letter widths, spacing, kerning and much else can benefit, and even the different widths of a single letter can be unitized. My colleague Jörg Drees showed this well in his dissertation project at Plantin. He drew a typeface in which some letters came in several widths, for example a normal, a wide and an extra-wide a, e, n or m, each differing by one unit. Because the variants step in whole units shared by every character in the font, justification can easily be achieved, with little or no extra room added to the word spaces.

For the non-believers: if you can set letter widths and spacing quickly this way, why leave it to the whims of eyeballing? If you insist on using the eye as the ultimate judge, you can start with the grid to speed up the work and to control the design details and then fine-tune by eye as a final stage, if you still find it necessary.

Personally, I side with the system. I am a pattern-head, and Guapísima is unitized.

A few things about me

Sebastian Boroș (1980) born
in Târgu Mureș, Romania

I got into graphic design by accident, which is how I suspect most good things happen. I was studying journalism at the time (1999), and I’d had a feeling for a while that journalism wasn’t going to be my life. Via dial-up internet I found out graphic design was a thing people actually did for a living, and that was the end of journalism.

The 1990s and early 2000s were crazy times in Eastern Europe. Nobody knew what the future will bring, but everyone was buzzing around with plans and businesses. Romania was flooded with Western magazines, and I took great delight in studying their mastheads and layouts, which looked very different from the publications of the communist era. Soon, type became an object of admiration and intrigue, and I found much joy in researching type.

I remember walking into a newspaper shop in Bucharest’s Gara de Nord to buy cigarettes and noticing, on the shelf, the beautiful type of the Intelligent Life magazine masthead. I soon learned it was set in dtl Fleischmann, Erhard Kaiser’s digital revival of the most expertly cut types of Johan Michael Fleischmann. The text of the articles was set in Fleischmann too, and I wondered how a typeface so intricate and idiosyncratic can be both a cool display type and such a readable text face. Fleischmann and his reanimators must have been magicians.

Typophile, FontShop, Linotype, urw++, teff and Dutch Type Library became places I liked to explore. Along came the heroes: Doyald Young, Hermann Zapf, Gerrit Noordzij, Frank Blokland, Adrian Frutiger, Giambattista Bodoni, Miklós Kis. When it came to type-related books, Hyphen Press was one of the best publishing houses out there.

In the meantime, graphic design was a great source of satisfaction. In our studio (Blinker) in Bucharest we have done projects for sabMiller (now ab InBev), Siemens, Institut Français, Christian Tour, among the well-known names, and a myriad of new businesses that were spawning in Romania and needed visual identities.

I took my first step towards a formal education in type with the Expert Class Type Design in Antwerp in 2024, under Frank Blokland, at the Plantin Institute of Typography.

If you happen to visit Antwerp I recommend you visit The Museum Plantin Moretus (mpm). It’s the Silicon Valley of the 16th century, the place where the new technology of the printed book took the form of a world-class industry under the skilled management of Christophe Plantin’s family and later carried out through generations until the 19th century. There are also live printing demonstrations with a replica of a 16th century press and also portraits and engravings by Rubens. The museum is also the home of the Plantin Institute of Typography, the institution that organized the course. My class was Frank’s last at the mpm, but now he organizes a new course called The Origins of Type Frameworks. More about this here.

Frank is one of those teachers that give their students the confidence to find their own voice. During his classes there’s always a fresh air of exploration based on reason, where established wisdom is questioned and everyone is encouraged to use their own logic and eyes during their research.

His course was also an excellent occasion to make new friends. Being self-taught, that was something I really did not encounter many times. To meet like-minded type people was a welcome side-effect of the experience.

I am currently working on expanding and refining the comprehensive revival of both text and display version of Gerónimo Antonio Gil’s types, among two other typefaces I intend to release in 2027.

I also work on custom type projects. For bigger brands this is usually the cheaper solution as it does away with complex licensing issues, present or future. It is also the most elegant option as it adds a bespoke visual dimension to the identity. If you think your brand could use a custom typeface designed by an expert, please drop me a line at seboros@gmail.com
